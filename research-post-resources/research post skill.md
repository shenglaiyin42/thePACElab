# PACE research to blog

## Purpose

Turn a research paper into an accessible story about what was learned, why it matters, and what it enables readers or researchers to do next. Generate interest in the original paper without reproducing its full results or manuscript structure. Simplify the explanation, not the evidence or uncertainty.

Write for interested readers without specialist training. Use a scientific, robust, approachable voice. Keep the prose concise and natural in both languages. Do not manufacture excitement, quotations, personal experiences, or claims of novelty.

## Read the right material

Use the latest author-edited draft as the starting text when revising. Use the manuscript and its figures as the factual authority. Use an author-supplied Chinese abstract as a terminology and meaning reference, not as a requirement to reuse academic sentence structures. Treat journal instructions as destination-specific requirements or guidance according to their actual wording.

Before drafting, establish the study type, scope, evidence, publication status, requested outputs, preferred title, explicit comments, and two to four principal messages. Reuse information already provided. Do not ask the author to repeat it. When details are absent, make a bounded draft and identify only the unresolved items that affect accuracy or release.

A newer draft controls editorial choices but does not authorize unsupported scientific claims. When drafts or sources conflict, identify the discrepancy and resolve it from the supplied evidence or flag it for the author. Do not silently repair inconsistent results by guessing. Do not replace the author's framing with general knowledge.

For file work, use the available file tools or actual mounted files. Inspect the images rather than relying only on captions. Follow the host environment's document and PDF instructions when handling those formats. Do not infer file paths from display names.

For the learned example, read [the conversion record](references/worked-example.md). The authoritative examples are the English and Chinese files under `examples/avian-influenza/`, not earlier drafts. They are private reference materials, not permission to distribute unpublished research publicly.

## Select the story before writing

Build a brief internal evidence map. For each proposed message, record its source section or figure, the supported claim, its main caveat, and why a non-specialist should care. Keep this working map out of the public post.

Extract the motivating question, a short explanation of what the researchers did, a few decisive findings, their biological or methodological meaning, and the practical or conceptual implications. Retain the sample size or study scope when it anchors the story. Keep essential limitations wherever they affect the meaning of a claim.

Usually leave out exhaustive subgroup counts, full variable lists, equations, software inventories, detailed screening procedures, and a long catalogue of limitations. Include a technical detail only when the reader needs it to understand a central result. Do not force management recommendations onto a paper that supports only a conceptual or methodological contribution.

Prioritize the author's stated messages. In the worked example these were complementary methods and decisions, interacting host communities under environmental change, and integration across scales and biological levels. These are case-specific messages, not compulsory themes for future papers.

Distinguish findings from explanations and recommendations. A review's frequency counts describe its included literature, not the effectiveness of a method or the prevalence of a biological process. Correlation is not causation. A scenario is not a forecast. A less-used method is not necessarily better. A promising integration is not a validated improvement unless the paper demonstrates it.

## Organize the English post

Use a short, descriptive, question-led or concept-led title. Retain an author-approved title unless a correction is needed or a change is requested. Do not copy the full paper title merely because it is available.

Open with a short paragraph that works as a standalone summary for the post. Prefer a broad question and an understandable takeaway. Do not label it “Abstract” or duplicate the paper's abstract. About 40 to 65 English words is a useful PACE default, not a journal requirement.

Follow with one or two short paragraphs explaining the wider problem and what this particular study did. For a review, make clear that the authors synthesized existing studies. For an experiment, explain the comparison. For a new method, explain the problem it addresses and the evidence used to evaluate it.

Develop the story through roughly three to five informative subheadings. Organize by concepts and reader questions rather than Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion. Within a section, move from a finding to a plain explanation, then an implication or limitation. Use a concrete, source-supported example where it makes an abstract issue understandable.

End with the useful takeaway, its boundary conditions, and the next step supported by the study. Avoid repeating every section. Finish with a brief linked pointer to the paper when its article URL is confirmed. Use the lightweight [English scaffold](templates/article.en.md) only as a guide, not a rigid structure.

## Apply the house language rules

Use short, connected paragraphs, each with one main job. Two to four sentences often work well. Most paragraphs in the example contain about 40 to 80 words, but do not sacrifice clarity to a numerical target. Prefer concrete verbs, familiar words, and explicit subjects. Use “we” naturally for the authors' actual work.

Explain indispensable technical terms on first use. Replace unnecessary specialist labels with descriptions of what happens. For example, “interactions among species” may work better than “interspecific interactions,” and “studies that look ahead” may work better than “prospective work.” Keep a precise term when simplifying it would change the science.

Use relevant keywords naturally in the title, opening, and conclusion. Repetition should sustain the main argument, not create redundant paragraphs or keyword stuffing.

Avoid colons wherever a sentence or comma works. Avoid parenthetical dashes wherever commas or separate sentences work. When a dash is necessary, use a short dash, an en dash (–), not an em dash (—). Preserve normal hyphens in compound words such as “decision-making” and “cross-scale.” Preserve punctuation that belongs to an exact publication title, DOI, URL, or code. Do not weaken grammar by replacing a necessary sentence break with a comma splice.

Retain uncertainty. Do not turn “may,” “could,” or “is associated with” into a definite effect. Do not imply that research gaps demonstrate biological unimportance. Avoid unsupported superlatives, novelty claims, guarantees, and causal language.

When editing an author draft, make the smallest changes that fix grammar, factual inconsistency, terminology, or flow. Preserve the author's priorities and voice. Resolve inline editorial tags, then remove them from the public files. Never invent a quotation or anecdote to make a post more conversational.

## Control length

For Methods Blog, the guidance supplied in this project suggests 750 to 1,500 English words and allows flexibility. A useful PACE working target is about 900 to 1,200 words, including captions and headings. The final worked example contains 1,121 words under the package's counting method.

Shorten by removing duplicate explanations and secondary findings, not by omitting qualifiers needed for scientific accuracy. An expanded explanation should replace lower-priority detail rather than simply lengthen the post. A Chinese translation should preserve the same message and reading burden, not match an English word count. Report Chinese character counts separately when useful.

Count visible text without Markdown syntax, image alt text, or URL strings. State whether the reported total includes headings, captions, and the related-study note. Do not mistake a three-page example for a journal page limit.

## Select and explain figures

Choose figures after the narrative is clear. Start with one or two strong visuals, and add another only when it does a distinct explanatory job. The user may specify figures initially, but reassess their relevance after substantial revisions.

For each visual, ask which nearby claim it supports, whether a beginner can understand its main message, and whether the image remains legible at the intended size. Remove a redundant or overly dense figure instead of keeping it to satisfy an arbitrary count. Prefer original research figures or appropriately licensed photographs. An optional infographic must add clarity, not repeat the entire paper.

Cropping a relevant panel is acceptable only when it preserves its values, axes, units, scale, legend, and meaning. Do not invent replacement data or recreate a quantitative figure with generative imagery. Record the original-to-blog figure mapping in working notes, not in confusing public figure labels.

Number figures in order of appearance within the blog, beginning with Figure 1 or 图1. Update every text callout, caption, alt description, and asset filename together. Do not retain manuscript figure numbers or an orphan panel suffix such as “1A” when the blog presents a standalone figure.

Place each image near its first substantive discussion. Write a caption that states the point, explains how to read the visual, and prevents the most likely misinterpretation. Explain overlapping categories or multiple contributions from one study where relevant. A ribbon diagram of literature categories is not a map of transmission routes. A frequency chart is not a performance comparison.

Use real local image assets with relative paths, preferably `figures/figure-1.png`. Link the displayed image to the full-resolution image when the platform supports it. Supply meaningful alt text. Keep numbering and asset choice consistent across languages. Translate captions and alt text. Do not silently redraw English chart labels in Chinese or alter an original figure.

Verify permissions, credits, and destination-specific image policies. A repository name alone does not establish reuse permission. The editor's no-AI cover instruction in this project concerns cover submissions. Do not portray an AI image as non-AI or assume that this cover instruction establishes every blog-image policy. Do not add a generated image merely for decoration.

## Use hyperlinks rather than manuscript citations

For a Methods Blog-style post, replace repeated numbered citations with a few descriptive hyperlinks at relevant claims. Link the central study on its first meaningful mention once the article link exists, and optionally at the closing pointer. Link supporting research only where it genuinely helps the reader. Do not add a formal bibliography by default.

Use supplied, confirmed URLs. When a link or outside claim needs verification, consult the original source and distinguish that verification from the manuscript's findings. Do not invent publication metadata or treat a dataset DOI as the article DOI. Do not search for or expose an unpublished manuscript just to make a draft look complete.

For an accepted paper without an article link, retain a clear editorial placeholder in the submission draft and identify it in the handoff. Before public release, replace it with the confirmed publication link and remove pending-publication language. “Ready to send to the editor” and “ready to publish publicly” are separate states.

## Produce the Chinese version

Use the corrected, approved English as the content source. English-first drafting is the default. Produce both versions in the same pass when explicitly requested, translating from the corrected English rather than a superseded Chinese draft.

Write natural Simplified Chinese. Preserve the argument, emphasis, examples, numerical values, caveats, headings' functions, hyperlinks, and figure numbering. Adjust sentence order and split sentences when needed. Do not translate English clause structure mechanically or add an extra scientific claim to make a passage sound smoother.

Use the author-supplied Chinese abstract and [translation notes](references/translation-notes.md) to keep terminology consistent. Preserve distinctions such as population versus community, biological level versus spatial scale, infection versus transmission, and association versus cause. Translate “may,” “could,” and “can” according to their actual certainty and context, not through a fixed word substitution.

Compare the two versions section by section. Verify that neither strengthens a claim or omits a limitation. Make subsequent substantive corrections in both languages. Exact paragraph counts are not mandatory, but a missing section, example, qualifier, or caption must be explained or fixed.

## Package readable files

Create only the deliverables requested. For a bilingual journal-blog package, the normal set is one English Markdown file, one Chinese Markdown file, an English Word submission when requested, and the selected original image assets. Bundle these in a ZIP when it helps preserve image paths. Do not add a separate excerpt, social blurb, or multiple competing “final” versions unless asked.

Keep Markdown clean. Use headings, prose, hyperlinks, image links, and captions. Do not embed HTML, CSS, style blocks, or website-configuration comments in reading copies. Do not put four leading ASCII spaces before paragraphs, which can turn prose into code. The PACE example uses two Unicode spaces for first-line indentation. Some renderers may collapse them, so do not promise exact Markdown layout.

For Word, apply true paragraph-format indentation and remove the Markdown indent characters. Use compact spacing with no extra empty paragraphs between body paragraphs. Give subheadings a modest gap, not a full blank line. Embed figures, keep captions with their figures, preserve aspect ratios, and use actual clickable hyperlinks rather than raw Markdown. Follow the host document skill, render all pages, inspect them, and correct clipping, unreadable labels, or broken pagination. See the example's measured layout in [the conversion record](references/worked-example.md). Those settings are a lab baseline, not journal requirements.

Generate Word from the same corrected English text as the Markdown. Check for text, figure, and hyperlink parity. Do not independently rewrite during export. Provide an explicit download link for every user-facing deliverable. Do not claim to have installed this skill, published the post, emailed the journal, or saved project memory unless an actual authorized action has done so.

## Final quality gate

Run the available automated checks and complete [the release checklist](templates/release-checklist.md). The supplied script supports the plain Markdown conventions used here and basic DOCX parity. It does not establish factual correctness, translation quality, rights, or live-link validity.

```bash
python scripts/check_blog.py --en path/to/post.en.md --zh path/to/post.zh.md --docx path/to/submission.en.docx
```

For a public-release check, add `--release`. An unresolved article-link placeholder should then fail the release gate, not be silently removed. The example intentionally retains this placeholder because the paper was not online when the files were finalized.

Before handoff, confirm source support, author priorities, plain language, uncertainty, sensible length, figure integrity and numbering, bilingual equivalence, working local assets, matching Word text, and removal of comments or obsolete citations. Report important unresolved issues briefly. Keep the handoff short and prioritize file links.

## Use and update this skill

Treat this `SKILL.md` as the editable master for the writing workflow. The accompanying ZIP is a matching working copy of this master together with its supporting references, templates, examples, and checker. The conversion summary is background only, not a competing set of instructions. For a new post, provide the package with the research manuscript, figures, author comments, and destination guidance. The package does not automatically install itself or persist across conversations.

When the author asks to update the skill from later conversations, read the latest supplied master and the relevant conversation or comments. Extract explicit, reusable editorial preferences and lessons. Distinguish standing preferences from instructions for one particular article. Do not make a paper's subject, examples, figure count, or incidental wording a universal rule. Where the author's newer general instruction explicitly changes an earlier one, update the affected rule and remove contradictory wording rather than accumulating competing instructions.

Revise the relevant sections of this master, then update affected supporting materials so they remain consistent. Preserve completed example articles as historical references rather than silently rewriting them. Do not turn optional journal suggestions into requirements or describe old supplied guidance as freshly verified policy.

For skill updates, return only two downloads unless the author requests more. Provide the updated editable `SKILL.md` and a rebuilt skill ZIP containing that exact file and all required supporting materials. Explain substantive changes briefly in the response. Do not create separate conversion reports, update documents, or multiple competing master files. The author can annotate this master or supply new conversation excerpts with it for the next update.
